# Six-Pager Memo Format

The six-pager is Amazon's standard format for complex strategic topics requiring detailed analysis and discussion. It originated in the early 2000s when Amazon needed to manage rapid growth and executive capacity constraints. Six pages emerged as "long enough for depth, and short enough to force clarity." PowerPoint is banned for decision-making contexts.

## Purpose

Use a six-pager when:

- The topic requires comprehensive understanding across multiple dimensions
- A logical narrative flow is needed to build a persuasive argument
- Supporting data, research, or case studies must be presented
- Multiple stakeholders need shared context before making a decision
- The decision has significant impact and requires thorough analysis

## Structure

### Introduction

State the purpose of the document and provide essential context. The reader should understand within the first two paragraphs: what this document is about, why it matters, and what decision or action is being requested. Do not bury the ask.

### Problem Statement

Define the customer problem or business opportunity with precision. Include:

- Who is affected (specific customer segment, not "users")
- What the problem is (concrete description, not abstract)
- How severe it is (quantified impact: time lost, revenue at risk, error rates)
- Why it matters now (what changed, what is the urgency)

### Tenets

Optional but common. List the principles guiding the proposal. Tenets should create tension — a tenet that nobody would disagree with is not useful. Good tenets force prioritization: "We optimize for reliability over latency" is useful because someone could reasonably argue the opposite.

### Current State

Describe how things work today. Include metrics, customer feedback, and operational data. This section establishes the baseline against which the proposal is measured. Be honest about what is working, not only what is broken.

### Proposed Solution

Describe the end-state experience in narrative form. Walk through the customer journey or operational flow after the change. Explain the approach at the level of detail appropriate for the audience — enough to evaluate feasibility, not so much that it becomes a technical specification.

### Alternatives Considered

Describe other approaches evaluated and why they were rejected. This demonstrates rigor and preempts "why didn't you consider X?" questions. Be fair to alternatives — presenting straw men erodes credibility.

### Risks and Mitigations

Identify what could go wrong and how each risk is addressed. Distinguish between risks that are mitigated by the plan and risks that remain open. For open risks, state the monitoring approach and trigger conditions for changing course.

### Resource Requirements

What is needed to execute: team, timeline, dependencies, budget. Be specific about what is being asked for.

### Key Metrics and Success Criteria

Define how success is measured. Include leading indicators (early signals) and lagging indicators (ultimate outcomes). Specify baselines, targets, and timelines.

### Next Steps

Concrete actions with owners and dates. The reader should leave the meeting knowing exactly what happens next.

## Formatting Rules

1. **Narrative prose only.** No bullet points in the body. No slide-style formatting. Each paragraph builds on the previous one, leading to a coherent argument. Tables and charts are permitted sparingly for data presentation, but they supplement the narrative — they do not replace it.
2. **Exactly six pages.** Not five, not seven. The constraint is deliberate. If the document is under six pages, the analysis is likely incomplete. If it exceeds six pages, the writing is not tight enough.
3. **Default styles.** Use standard formatting. No decorative fonts, unusual spacing, or graphics designed to fill space. Dense pages are better than sparse ones.
4. **No author names.** The document is evaluated on the strength of its ideas, not on the seniority of its author.
5. **Appendix is allowed.** Supporting data, detailed calculations, and reference material can go in an appendix beyond the six pages. The six-page body must stand alone — the appendix is for readers who want to go deeper.

## Reading Protocol

Six-pager meetings begin with 20–25 minutes of silent reading. All attendees read, highlight, and take notes before any discussion begins. After reading:

1. General feedback is solicited. Senior attendees speak last to avoid anchoring.
2. Detailed line-by-line discussion follows.
3. Meeting minutes capture feedback and decisions.
4. The document is revised and may go through multiple review cycles.

Teams typically produce ten or more drafts before leadership approval.

## Quality Standards

A strong six-pager demonstrates:

- **Mastery**: Deep understanding of every aspect of the topic
- **Realism**: Optimistic but honest assessment, not rose-colored
- **Data-driven**: Firm grasp of metrics and evidence, with every claim supported
- **Risk awareness**: Clear understanding of what could go wrong and conditions for success or failure
- **Customer focus**: The customer problem and benefit are central, not an afterthought

## Common Mistakes

- Using bullet points instead of narrative prose
- Burying the recommendation — state the ask early
- Disguising assumptions as facts (label assumptions explicitly)
- Presenting weak alternatives to make the preferred option look better
- Optimistic timelines that assume best-case scenarios throughout
- Missing the "so what" — every paragraph must earn its place
- Over-polishing prose at the expense of substance
