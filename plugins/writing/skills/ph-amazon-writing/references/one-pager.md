# One-Pager Memo Format

A one-pager is a concise document summarizing the critical details of a concept, project, product launch, or business plan on a single page. Use it for straightforward topics that need a quick decision or for fast evaluation of an idea.

## Purpose

Use a one-pager when:

- The topic is well-defined and does not require extensive analysis
- You need a quick decision from leadership
- The audience is executives who need to understand the essentials immediately
- A six-pager would be disproportionate to the scope of the decision
- You need to align a group before a discussion (approximately 5 minutes of silent reading)

## Structure

### TL;DR

Two to three sentences capturing the entire document. State the problem, the proposal, and the recommendation. A reader who only reads this section should understand what is being asked and why.

### Context / Why

Establish why this document exists and why the reader should care. What changed? What is the problem or opportunity? Ground it in data: customer impact, revenue at risk, operational cost, or competitive pressure. Avoid starting with solutions — establish the problem first.

### What (Scope)

Describe the proposal. What are you proposing to do? Be specific about scope boundaries — what is included and what is explicitly excluded. The reader should understand the end-state customer or operational experience.

### How

Describe the approach at an appropriate level of detail. This is not a technical specification — it is enough for the reader to evaluate feasibility and identify risks. If multiple solution options exist, present them briefly with a recommendation.

### Key Assumptions and Risks

List the critical assumptions underlying the proposal. For each assumption, note the risk level (High / Medium / Low) and how it will be validated. Call out what you know versus what you are assuming.

### Next Steps

Concrete actions with owners and dates. End with a clear call to action: what decision is needed, and what happens after the decision is made.

## Writing Rules

1. **Exactly one page.** The constraint forces you to distill to what matters. If you cannot fit the essentials on one page, you either do not understand the topic well enough or the topic requires a six-pager.
2. **Lead with the main point.** Do not build up to the recommendation — state it in the TL;DR and then support it. Executives should understand why they should care within the first paragraph.
3. **Narrative prose, not bullets.** Write in complete sentences with logical flow from beginning to middle to end. The document tells a story.
4. **Set reader expectations.** State upfront what the document covers and what it does not cover, and why the reader should engage with it.
5. **Dense formatting.** Default styles, no decorative spacing. A full page of content is better than a half-page with generous margins.
6. **FAQ appendix if needed.** If one page is insufficient for supporting data, add an FAQ appendix. The one-pager body must stand alone.

## Seven-Step Writing Process

1. **Writing is reading.** Reread your draft as if you have no context. Test with a colleague to ensure the document stands alone without implicit assumptions.
2. **Set reader expectations.** State upfront what the document covers and does not cover.
3. **Start, middle, end.** Begin with context and problems, move through solutions, conclude with assumptions, dependencies, and remaining risks.
4. **Edit aggressively.** Eliminate unnecessary language, colloquialisms, weasel words, and imprecise terms. Be as concise as possible without sacrificing clarity.
5. **Use default formatting.** No distracting spacing or graphics.
6. **Include TL;DR / BLUF.** The executive summary captures the key message upfront for readers who skim.
7. **Add FAQ appendix if needed.** Supporting questions and data when one page is insufficient.

## Common Mistakes

- Burying the main point instead of leading with it
- Using excessive technical jargon inaccessible to the audience
- Employing imprecise language ("might," "could," "possibly") without labeling uncertainty
- Lacking a clear recommendation — every one-pager should end with a decision request
- Starting with solutions before establishing the customer problem
- Using bullet-point lists as a substitute for narrative reasoning
