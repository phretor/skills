# Visuals and Delivery

Used after message clarity is locked. Never use these as a first step.

## Visual strategy

**Rule:** as many slides as your story needs. No more, no less. Your story dominates the visuals.

### Code

- Never show the whole snippet. Strip everything not essential to the point.
- Default to 2–4 lines per slide.
- Highlight the operative tokens with a colored box. The rest is context.
- If the audience is interested in more, they will reach out. Trust that.

### Diagrams

- Don't focus on making diagrams look good. Focus on making them *useful for the story*.
- A complex diagram dropped on a slide overwhelms the audience: "Where do I begin? Top-right? Top-left?" Their brain looks away.
- **Reveal incrementally.** Build the diagram with the audience. Duplicate the slide N times (N reveal steps); in each copy, delete everything but the part you're talking about. No animations needed.
- Alternative: cover everything except the active region with rectangles in the background color.

### Animations

- Only if they serve the narrative. The example that works: arrows showing instructions "leaking" from a virtual CPU to a physical one. The animation *is* the message.
- Decoration animations (spinning bullets, fly-ins) dilute attention.

### Video and demo

- A video or demo serves a purpose at a specific point. Don't insert it because "demos are expected."
- **Pre-record by default.** Pre-recording is not lazy; it's reliable. Live demo only if "this works live" is itself the message.
- Embed full-screen on the slide. Use the entire real estate.
- Auto-play or click-to-play — never fumble for the player on stage.
- Split long demos into bite-sized clips placed throughout the talk. Reinforces understanding while keeping attention high.

## Delivery — body and voice

Coach this **after** content is locked, never before.

### Body

- **Stage vs podium.** Podium with arms crossed, head down, reading from a laptop → narrator status. Use that if the talk demands it (poem, someone else's research). For your own research, *be* the story — center stage, full body.
- **Turning your back can be a power move.** If you've put text on a slide for the audience to read, or a video for them to watch, joining the audience by facing the screen is consistent — and they will read because you do. Mid-ground (facing audience while pointing at unread text) breaks the consistency between body and speech.
- **Unconscious movements.** Tapping, swinging, pacing at a regular rhythm soothes the speaker and annoys the audience. Record rehearsals to surface them.

### Voice modulation

- Monotone puts people asleep — full stop.
- Up-down modulation creates and releases tension, suspense, joy. Match modulation to the narrative arc.
- Voice is your instrument, not a sound card.

The MFCC-delta intuition: the time-derivatives of spectral shape capture how quickly your timbre is changing. "Engaging" speeches have large deltas; "flat" speeches have small ones. You don't need to measure it — just *vary*.

### Stage management

- Eye contact with as many people as possible. Don't fixate on one.
- For the bright lights: look at the audience for a few seconds before you start — let your eyes adjust to the room, not the slide.
