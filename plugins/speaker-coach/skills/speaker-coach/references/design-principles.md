# Design Principles — Backwards from the Last Slide

Forward-design discipline. These are the rules `design` mode enforces. Cite the principle name when invoking it.

## 1. Start from the last slide

Don't open PowerPoint. Don't pick a template. Start from the **takeaway** — the single thing you want the audience to remember on the way out. Everything else is engineered backward to serve that goal.

> *Working Backwards* (Bryar & Carr): "We start with the desired customer experience and work backward from that." Replace "customer" with "audience."

## 2. The 280-character tweet

The takeaway must fit in a tweet. If it doesn't, the speaker doesn't know what the talk is about yet.

**Quality bar — must satisfy all three:**

- **Actionable** — can the audience act on it?
- **Memorable** — can they repeat it over lunch?
- **True** — does it hold up under scrutiny?

Coach behavior: iterate ruthlessly until the takeaway clears the bar. Do not proceed to sub-messages until it does.

## 3. Three-plus sub-messages (topic sentences)

The takeaway decomposes into 3 (sometimes 4) supporting topic sentences. Each must be:

- A self-contained sentence (a *topic sentence*, in the rhetorical sense — content-rich, depends on no external concepts).
- Distinct (no overlap with siblings).
- Necessary (drop one → takeaway no longer supported).

If the speaker can't produce 3 distinct sub-messages, the takeaway is wrong. Iterate.

## 4. Audience subset — target the room you want, not the room you have

The target audience is **not** everyone in the room. It's the subset the speaker chose to serve. Decide who. Different audiences read different signals:

- **Prospect customers** → measure trustworthiness; focus on impact and mitigation; skip "how I found this."
- **Press** → look for "so-what?"; need relatable examples; avoid speculation.
- **Peers** → can replicate the work; will ask the toughest questions; anticipate the weak spots.

Coach behavior: name the subset explicitly. "Everyone" is a wrong answer.

## 5. Narrative arcs — never a flat story

A talk is a stack of narrative arcs, not a linear list. Each arc: tension build → climax → resolution → setup for the next arc. Energy must move.

Coach behavior: when reviewing a draft outline, identify each arc and check that one ends where the next begins.

## 6. Visuals support narrative; narrative does not support visuals

If the speaker can't say *which sub-message a slide serves*, the slide is decoration. Cut it.

See `visuals-and-delivery.md` for incremental-reveal techniques.

## 7. Speaker notes are the real deck

Most of what makes a presentation good lives in the speaker notes and outside the slide deck. Slides are billboards; the notes are the talk.

## 8. Hard "no" list

The coach refuses to help with these — and names them when the speaker tries:

- **No template-first** — templates trick you into thinking design is filling in blanks.
- **No "let me thank you for being here"** — filler intros are stage-fright coping mechanisms.
- **No "let me talk a bit about myself"** — credentials emerge from content, not from a whoami slide.
- **No agenda slide** — the agenda spoils the story. Great books don't open with the table of contents.
- **No live demo by default** — pre-record unless the *purpose* is "prove this works live." Split big demos into bite-sized clips.
